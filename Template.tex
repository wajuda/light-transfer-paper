\begin{abstract}
Nighttime Image Enhancement (NIE) aims to improve the visibility of weakly illuminated images. Existing supervised methods rely on low/normal-light pairs captured from short/long exposure variation, which generalize poorly to real nighttime scenes. To overcome this, we propose the Reversible Lighting Transfer Network (RLTNet), which enhances a nighttime image by transferring the illumination of a weakly paired daytime reference. RLTNet maps each pair of daytime and nighttime images into a compact latent space with a reversible encoder that guarantees exact invertibility without a separately trained decoder. In this latent space, a Regularized Whitening and Coloring Transform (RegWCT) aligns the feature covariance of night images with that of day images. A spatial-aware refiner then adaptively blends the stylized output with the nighttime input to avoid overexposure. Experiments on collected Daytime-Nighttime image pairs (DNP) and other public night image dataset show the superior performance of our method.




%This paper introduces the Adaptive Lighting Transfer Network (ALTNet), a domain-tailored framework for Nighttime Image Enhancement (NIE). The illumination characteristics of the daytime references are transferred to the nighttime images for illumination enhancement. Our method is flexible enough to allow the referenced daytime image to play a role both in the training and testing phases, and to permit different objects or humans occurring in the scene without the need of exact pixel alignment. Extensive experiments on various benchmark datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed ALTNet. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}




Nighttime Image Enhancement (NIE) aims to improve the brightness and visibility of weakly illuminated images. The ideal outcome is to produce a result that appears as if it was captured under normal lighting conditions, while preserving the original content \cite{surveyli}. NIE has been applied in various fields, including nighttime surveillance, autonomous driving, and photo editing \cite{surveykbs}.




%Traditional methods for the LLIE problem can be categorized primarily into histogram equalization-based approaches \cite{he} and Retinex-based approaches \cite{retinex}. Histogram equalization-based methods aim to adjust the original pixel distribution. On the other hand, Retinex-based methods are founded on the Retinex theory, which posits that an image can be decomposed into an illumination component and a reflection component. Conventional methods often struggle with diverse lighting conditions and exhibit limited performance.

\section{DATASET PREPARATION} \label{sec:dataset}

We introduce a new weakly paired dataset, whose image pairs are scene-consistent but semantically variant. Specifically, the image pairs are taken in the same scene during the day and at night, respectively. Thus, the image pairs are able to reflect the real degradation problem. Besides, dynamic objects (e.g., vehicles, pedestrians) may appear, disappear, or change positions at different times. It is impossible and unnecessary to perfectly match them at the pixel level, which has greatly reduced the difficulty of collection. In short, we refer to this readily available dataset as the DNP (Daytime-Nighttime image Pairs). We used a mobile phone camera with default parameters to collect 344 pairs of images (317 for training and 27 for testing) from different scenes. %\subsection{Reversible Lighting Transfer Network (RLTNet)}
%\label{sec:RLTNet}

The architecture diagram of the proposed RLTNet is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:structure}. It consists of three components: reversible network, Regularized Whitening and Coloring Transform (RegWCT) and the Refiner network.

%It consists of three components: a \emph{reversible network} that produces invertible latent representations of images, a Regularized Whiten--Colorize Transfer (RegWCT) that transfers lighting in the latent space, and a refiner network that adaptively blends the stylized output with the night input.

\subsection{Reversible Network}
\label{sec:revnet}
%Nighttime image enhancement (NIE) requires the enhanced result to preserve the original content faithfully. Generic encoder--decoder networks tend to lose fine details and produce the distortion, which makes them more suitable for artistic style transfer than for realistic enhancement. In contrast, 
The reversible network guarantees an exact reconstruction of the input from its latent represetation, eliminating the need for a separately trained decoder and ensuring that the content is perfectly preserved by construction.



Concretely, the reversible encoder takes a daytime or nighttime image $I \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W}$ and splits it into two halves along the channel dimension, namely $\bm{x}_1$ and $\bm{x}_2$:
\begin{equation}
%\begin{aligned}
    \bm{z}_2 = \bm{x}_2 + {E}_1(\bm{x}_1), 
    \bm{z}_1 = \bm{x}_1 + {E}_2(\bm{z}_2),
%\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where ${E}_1$ and ${E}_2$ are extractor modules. The forward pass produces the latent features $\bm{Z}$. The inverse pass recovers the input analytically,
\begin{equation}
%\begin{aligned}
    \bm{x}_1 = \bm{z}_1 - {E}_2(\bm{z}_2), 
    \bm{x}_2 = \bm{z}_2 - {E}_1(\bm{x}_1),
%\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
without any additional learnable parameters. Each extractor ${E}$ consists of three stacked convolutional layers as Fig.~\ref{fig:structure} (c). 

%The first $3 \times 3$ conv reduces the channel dimension to a low-dimensional bottleneck to lower the parameter count, the second is a dilated $3 \times 3$ conv that enlarges the receptive field, and the third $3 \times 3$ conv projects the channels back to the original dimension.

Before the reversible network, we feed the nighttime image $I_{\text{night}}$ into a frozen DCE++ \cite{zero-dceplus} illumination module to obtain an initial enhanced image $\widetilde{I}_{\text{night}}$. Pre-enhancing makes its distribution closer to the daytime one and stabilizes the subsequent latent-space transformation.

%, especially under heavy illumination degradation.

\subsection{Regularized Whitening and Coloring Transform}

After the reversible encoder, we obtain the latent codes $Z_{\text{day}}$ and $Z_{\text{night}}$ for the daytime and nighttime images, respectively. We then perform the lighting transfer with the Whitening and Coloring Transform (WCT) \cite{capvst}, which aligns the inter-channel covariance of the content feature with that of the style feature. 

%WCT consists of two steps: whitening the content feature to remove its original statistics and then coloring it with the target statistics.

Let $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times N}$ ($N = HW$) denote a reshaped latent feature and $Z_c = Z - {\mu}(Z)$ its centered version. Using the covariance notation of ${\Sigma}_Z = Z_c Z_c^{\!\top}$, the WCT is implemented as:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 Z_{\text{stylized}} 
    &= {L}_{Z_{\text{day}}}\, {L}_{Z_{\text{night}}}^{-1}\,(Z_{\text{night}} - \bm{\mu}(Z_{\text{night}})) + \bm{\mu}(Z_{\text{day}}),
\end{aligned}   
\end{equation}

where $LL^T$ is the Choleskey decomposition of covariance ${\Sigma}_Z$ ($L$ is a lower triangular matrix with positive diagonal elements).
%where $\bm{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times C}$ is orthogonal and $\lambda_i \geq 0$ are the eigenvalues. The transfer operation becomes
%\begin{equation}
%\begin{split}
%    Z_{\text{stylized}} 
%    &= \bm{\Sigma}_{Z_{\text{day}}}^{1/2}\, \bm{\Sigma}_{Z_{\text{night}}}^{-1/2}\,(Z_{\text{night}} - \bm{\mu}(Z_{\text{night}})) + \bm{\mu}(Z_{\text{day}}).
%\end{split}
%\end{equation}

It is easy to verify that $\bm{\Sigma}_{Z_{\text{stylized}}}$ equals $\bm{\Sigma}_{Z_{\text{day}}}$. Some eigenvalues of $\bm{\Sigma}_{Z_{\text{night}}}$ are close to zero, so that the whitening step amplifies noise and produces instability. To address this, we propose a RegWCT that stabilizes the transfer with two operations.

\noindent\textbf{Covariance shrinkage.}
Let ${\sigma^2} = \mathrm{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_Z)/C$ be the average variance. We mix the empirical covariance with a scaled identity $I$,
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}_Z = (1 - \alpha)\, \bm{\Sigma}_Z + \alpha\, {\sigma^2}\, \bm{I}, 
\end{equation}
which lifts near-zero eigenvalues and prevents excessive inverse-whitening gains.

\noindent\textbf{Transform-matrix gain clipping.}
The transform matrix that maps centered content features to style-colored features is
\begin{equation}
    \bm{T} ={L}_{Z_{\text{day}}}\, \widetilde{L}_{Z_{\text{night}}}^{-1}.
\end{equation}
We bound the spectral norm of $\bm{T}$ with the maximum singular value of $s_{max}$,
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{\bm{T}} = \bm{U}\, \mathrm{diag}\!\left(\min(\sigma_i,\; s_{\max})\right)\, \bm{V}^{\!\top},
\end{equation}
where $\bm{T} = \bm{U}\, \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_C)\, \bm{V}^{\!\top}$ is the SVD of $\bm{T}$. The final transform reads
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\text{stylized}} = \widehat{\bm{T}}\, (Z_{\text{night}} - \bm{\mu}(Z_{\text{night}})) + \bm{\mu}(Z_{\text{day}}).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Refiner Network}
\label{sec:alphanet}

A global WCT output often is prone to overexposure in certain areas, such as the areas of street lamps at night. To address this, we introduce a refiner that adaptively mixes the stylized $I_{stylized}$ and the initial enhanced night image $\widetilde{I}_{\text{night}}$. Refiner network is consist of a channel-wise branch using a Squeeze-and-Excitation block that re-weights feature channels, and a spatial-wise branch using stacked convolutions that captures local spatial context. The two branches are followed by a sigmoid, producing an attention map $\bm{\beta} \in [0, 1]^{1 \times H \times W}$. The final enhanced image is then reconstructed as:
\begin{equation}
    I_{\text{enhanced}} = \bm{\beta} \odot I_{\text{stylized}} + (1 - \bm{\beta}) \odot \widetilde{I}_{\text{night}},
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication.


\subsection{Loss}
In the weakly paired dataset, real daytime images have scenes similar to those of night images, but the specific objects can be different. Therefore, there is no pixel-level ground truth as in supervised learning. As discussed above, the NIE task can be seen as a kind of style transfer problem. The widely used $L_{style}$ and $I_{rec}$ are adopated: 
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
    L = L_{style} + \lambda L_{rec},
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where the VGG-19 network (from ReLu1$\_$1 to ReLu4$\_$1) is employed to obtain $L_{style}$.

\section{CONCLUSION}

In this paper, we proposed RLTNet for Nighttime Image Enhancement under a weakly-supervised setting. RLTNet operates entirely in a learned latent space: a reversible encoder produces invertible features, a Regularized WCT transfers the lighting from a daytime reference to a nighttime image stably, and a refiner adaptively blends the stylized output with the initially illuminated input. Extensive experiments on multiple datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of RLTNet. Our future work will focus on designing elaborate pixel-level supervision
to boost the detail restoration.
